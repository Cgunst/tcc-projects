Why this matters

Trinity Community Center is a former elementary school given new life as a home for many things at once: our chapel and pastor’s office, the community pantry, 4-H and meeting rooms, the cafeteria, the Great Room, and offices leased to tenants. Many people from the congregation and the wider community walk through these doors — and much of what they come for now depends, quietly, on the building’s technology: the Wi-Fi, the sound system, the computers that run services and meetings.

Our new community room — a roughly 50-by-50-foot gathering space — will be dedicated at a ceremony in mid-January 2027, with the annual congregational meeting to follow. That ceremony is our target: the goal is to have most of these projects completed by then, so the building works the way it should on the day we celebrate it.

Every project listed below was approved by the Property Use Committee as submitted, subject to funding. The plans are ready. What is still needed is the funding to carry them out — and that is where your help comes in.

Already under way — at no cost

Secure, separate networks — and a friendlier guest Wi-Fi

StatusApproved — in progress Estimated cost$0 — volunteer labor

Work that costs nothing but time is already happening. The building’s network is being reorganized so that staff, guests, AV equipment, and building systems each run on their own secure network — kept deliberately separate, the way locking the file cabinet is separate from locking the front door. A small mistake or a problem in one area can no longer spill into the others.

Guests will also soon see a simple welcome page with terms of use when they join the guest Wi-Fi — a friendly “hello” that also states, plainly, the rules that keep the network safe for everyone.

The projects, in priority order

These are listed in the order the committee has agreed they should be done. Each is ready to go as soon as it is funded.

All cost figures on this page are estimates, based on typical current U.S. retail prices as of November 2026; final costs may vary.

Project 1

Community room audio-visual system

StatusApproved — awaiting funding Estimated costEstimated $3,100–$3,900 (as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

What it is. The community room’s new 98-inch TV and existing surround speakers are currently run through a small stereo that was meant to be a stopgap. This project replaces it with a proper home-theater receiver (an Onkyo TX-RZ70), adds a powered subwoofer, and adds a modern, compact media PC (an Acemagic F2A) for playing music, slides, and videos. Everything is rack-mounted neatly in the console below the TV — one tidy cabinet instead of a tangle of gear. At current retail, the receiver runs about $1,700–$2,000, a good powered subwoofer about $300–$600, and the media PC about $1,100–$1,300.

Why it matters. This is the room we will dedicate in January. It will host the dedication ceremony, the annual meeting, and countless events after that — community gatherings, movies, presentations, and meetings that need to be heard clearly from the front row to the back corner. The stopgap stereo cannot do that room justice. This system can, and it is simple enough that volunteers can run it without calling for help.

What your gift makes possible

Clear speech and full music at the dedication service and in every gathering the room hosts for years to come — so someone in the last row hears every word as well as someone in the first.

Project 2

Chapel computer replacement

StatusApproved — awaiting funding Estimated costEstimated $600–$770 (as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

What it is. Replacing the chapel’s aging computer with a compact, modern mini PC (an Acemagic K5), along with a second 27-inch monitor. At current retail, the mini PC runs about $500–$570 and a good 27-inch monitor about $100–$200.

Why it matters. The chapel computer carries a growing share of our worship life — hymns and liturgy, slides, and recordings. The current one is aging, and an aging computer on a Sunday morning is a distraction nobody needs. The new machine does double duty in two ways that serve the whole building: it becomes the station where security footage can be reviewed when needed, and it opens the door to livestreaming services in the future — so members who are homebound, ill, or far from home can still worship with us. The second monitor makes it much easier for the person running the service to see what the congregation sees.

What your gift makes possible

Smoother, distraction-free services; a proper place to review building footage when it matters; and the foundation for sharing our services with members who cannot be here in person.

Project 3

Security cameras for entrances and parking

StatusApproved — awaiting funding Estimated costEstimated $2,400–$3,300 (as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

What it is. About five professional cameras covering the building entrances and the parking lot — a wide panoramic camera (UniFi G6 180), two all-weather dome cameras (UniFi G6 Dome), and two higher-grade dome cameras with optical zoom and enhanced night vision (UniFi G6 Pro Dome) — recording onto equipment the building already owns, with a dedicated 8-terabyte surveillance drive (Seagate SkyHawk 8TB) just for the recordings. Volunteers will do the installation, wiring it with solid-copper riser-rated network cable, and the tools have been donated — so every dollar given goes toward cameras, drive, and cable, not labor. At current U.S. retail: the G6 180 runs about $300–$420; the G6 Dome about $280–$330 each; the G6 Pro Dome about $500–$650 each; the SkyHawk 8 TB drive about $235–$390; and two 1,000-foot boxes of solid-copper riser cable about $170–$250 each.

Why it matters. Our building hosts young and old, evening and daytime, congregation and community. Cameras at the doors and lot are a quiet reassurance for everyone who uses them — and if something ever does go wrong, we will have the record needed to sort it out fairly.

Who may view footage. A written policy is part of the plan. Proposed — pending council adoption: viewing is limited to two or three people appointed by the council; requests to review footage go through the elders, and every viewing is logged; recordings are kept for about 30 days and then automatically overwritten unless tied to a specific incident; there is no audio recording and no live monitoring; and footage is shared with law enforcement only on formal request, through the elders. The camera system is being sized and placed with this policy in mind, so the protection of people’s privacy is designed in from the start — not added afterward.

What your gift makes possible

A safer entrance for every child at 4-H night, every guest at the pantry, and every car in the lot after an evening meeting — with clear rules that respect everyone’s privacy.

Project 4

Lockable network closet

StatusApproved — awaiting funding Estimated costEstimated $600–$1,100 (as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

What it is. The building’s network equipment currently sits on an open rack in a shared storage room. This project frames in and encloses that rack in a small, drywalled, lockable closet of its own, with a solid-core door. Volunteers will do the building work. At current home-center prices: framing lumber (studs run about $3.75–$4.50 each), drywall, joint compound, and paint; a solid-core prehung door about $230–$440; and a keyed lockset about $26–$70.

Why it matters. Every guest’s Wi-Fi, every meeting’s slides, and the systems that run the building pass through that rack. Today it is out in the open, where it can collect dust, take an accidental bump, or be unplugged by someone who didn’t know better — any of which could quietly interrupt the whole building. A framed, lockable closet protects the heart of the building’s technology and keeps the storage room usable for storage.

What your gift makes possible

Reliability: the connections everyone depends on, protected from dust, bumps, and accidents — and a closet door that locks, so only the right people ever touch it.

Project 5

Wi-Fi refresh

StatusApproved — awaiting funding Estimated costEstimated $2,200–$2,400 (as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

What it is. Replacing the building’s eleven older wireless access points with current Wi-Fi 7 models (Ubiquiti U7 Pro XG) for better coverage and capacity. A first U7 Pro XG is already installed and serving the Great Room. Per-unit × count: the access points run about $199–$219 each; the project covers the eleven replacement units (11 × $199–$219 = $2,189–$2,409).

Why it matters. Wi-Fi is the front door to almost everything that happens here now: pantry volunteers looking up resources, meeting attendees pulling up documents, guests in the Great Room, and the people who work in this building every day. The older units were good in their day, but a building this size, used this heavily, needs current equipment to reach every corner and carry everyone at once — especially on busy days when several rooms are in use at the same time.

What your gift makes possible

Fast, dependable Wi-Fi in every room of the building — for the pantry, the meetings, the tenants’ guests, and everyone in between.

Project 6

Door access control

StatusFuture project — not yet funded or scheduled Estimated costPreliminary: roughly $1,700–$3,400 (preliminary estimate, as of November 2026 — scope and hardware to be confirmed when planned; not included in the Projects 1–5 total)

What it is. Credentialed (card- or phone-based) entry for the building’s four exterior doors — the two main entrances and the two rear doors — replacing the passing-around of physical keys. The plan is built on Ubiquiti’s UniFi Access system: a door controller and card reader at each door, electrified locking hardware (strikes or maglocks as appropriate to each door), power supplies, door position sensors, and credentials. At current U.S. retail, per item × count: door controllers about $189–$199 each × 4; readers about $99–$359 each depending on model × 4; electric strikes about $100–$150 each × 4 (heavier-duty models for panic-bar doors can run up to about $430); power supplies about $13–$71 each × 4; door position sensors about $10–$60 each × 4; and an initial batch of access cards at about $30 per 10-pack.

Why it matters. Keys get copied, lost, and never quite returned. With credentialed entry, access can be granted to exactly the right people, changed when volunteers or tenants change, and removed in an instant when needed — and we can know when the building was opened and by whom. It is a future project — the figures here are preliminary, and it will return to the committee for full planning before anything is purchased — but it is shared here so the congregation can see where the building is headed.

Project 7

Building automation

StatusFuture project — not yet funded or scheduled Estimated costPreliminary: roughly $500–$700 (preliminary estimate, as of November 2026 — scope and hardware to be confirmed when planned; not included in the Projects 1–5 total)

What it is. Smart lighting and other building functions via Home Assistant, an open-source automation platform run on our own hardware rather than a cloud service. A starter phase: a Home Assistant hub, a Z-Wave/Zigbee coordinator radio, and about ten smart wall switches and dimmers for common-area lighting. At current U.S. retail, per item × count: the Home Assistant Green hub runs about $199; a coordinator radio about $30–$60 depending on model; smart switches and dimmers about $28–$44 each × 10.

Why it matters. A former school building is expensive to run, and lights left on in rooms nobody is using are a real part of that. This starter phase is simple stewardship: common-area lights that can be scheduled and switched off centrally instead of walked around and flipped by hand, plus a foundation that later phases can build on for other building functions. Like door access, it is a future project with preliminary figures — it will come back to the committee for full planning and costing.

Funding & how to give

Total needed: roughly $8,900–$11,500 for Projects 1–5 (estimated, as of November 2026; final costs may vary)

The total exceeds the initial $10,000 ask; projects proceed in priority order as funds allow. The low end of the estimate sits below $10,000; if costs land at the high end, the shortfall is roughly $1,500. No project is abandoned — later projects simply wait for their turn until funding catches up.

A note on the future projects. Projects 6 and 7 (door access and building automation) show preliminary figures for planning only; they are not part of the Projects 1–5 total or the current ask, and they will come back to the committee for full planning before any fundraising for them begins.

Every gift helps, at any size. Projects move forward in the priority order listed above, as funds come in — so partial funding isn’t a lesser gift; it simply moves the next project closer to done. If a specific project speaks to you, gifts can be designated for it; gifts may also be given toward the overall effort.

How to give. Give through the church’s regular giving: write “TCC Technology & AV Fund” on the memo line of a check made payable to the church, or mark your offering envelope the same way. To designate a gift for a specific project, add the project name (for example, “TCC Technology & AV Fund – Cameras”). (Giving wording pending confirmation by the church treasurer.)

Tax treatment. Gifts are made to the church, and the church’s usual giving statements and receipts apply. Please consult your tax advisor about deductibility. (Pending confirmation by the church treasurer.)

If we raise more than needed. Any funds left over after these projects are complete will be set aside for future Trinity Community Center technology and AV projects — including the future projects listed above — so nothing given is ever wasted.

Beyond direct gifts, a few friends of the Center have floated ideas such as a mid-winter chili cook-off and a silent auction. These are only ideas under consideration — nothing is planned yet — but they are ways the whole community could join in.
